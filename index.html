How subscribers interchange across the bundle

stickiness = services per household

The core mechanic: the more services a household holds, the harder it is to leave. A mobile-only customer churns easily; a household with mobile + fibre + security + a parent's fall-detection pendant almost never does. This is why the services war is the churn war.

The pull-through engine

  • →Fibre pulls TV — IP-based TELUS TV rides the PureFibre build
  • →Internet unlocks security — in BC/AB, SmartHome requires TELUS internet
  • →Mobility discounts everything — $10/mo off security, $10 off LivingWell for Mobility customers
  • →Health captures the aging household — the pendant keeps the whole family on TELUS

Why it beats Rogers & Bell

  • ◈Each added service drops churn probability materially
  • ◈TELUS can stack 6 lines on one bill; Rogers/Bell top out at ~4
  • ◈Health + security are contract-based (3–5 yr) — structurally sticky
  • ◈Cross-sell CAC is near-zero — selling line #4 to an existing customer costs a fraction of a new acquisition
◈ The CEO thesis in one line: "We don't win the household by having the cheapest single service — we win it by being the only one that can put wireless, fibre, TV, security, and a parent's medical pendant on one bill. Every line we add compounds the 0.91% churn advantage. My growth plan is depth-per-household, not just subscriber count."
◈ Subscriber-interchange stickiness is modelled directionally from public churn data and TELUS's disclosed bundle mechanics (security requiring internet in the West, Mobility cross-discounts). Exact per-line churn deltas aren't publicly disclosed — the structural relationship (more services → lower churn) is well-established; precise percentages are illustrative.

In-market vs BTL

the two price books

Every carrier runs two price books. In-market is what you see advertised — the acquisition offer, public, competitive, measurable. BTL (below-the-line) is what the retention desk hands out when you threaten to leave: discretionary credits, typically $20–$60/month for 6–24 months, never advertised, never in the price list. The gap between them is where margin quietly leaks.

Why BTL is structurally broken

  • ✗It rewards the wrong behaviour. The quiet, loyal customer subsidises the one who calls to cancel every year.
  • ✗It is invisible in the P&L. Spread across thousands of agent decisions, it never appears as a marketing line — it appears as ARPU decay.
  • ✗2026-67 blew it open. Carriers must now notify customers before a promo expires and show alternatives. The haggling cohort just became everyone.
  • ✗Zero switching cost. With fees banned and 4-hour porting, the threat behind every BTL call is now completely credible.

The TELUS answer — collapse the gap

  • ✓The 5-year price lock is the anti-BTL product. If the price never moves, there is nothing to call and renegotiate.
  • ✓Move spend above the line. A dollar in an advertised offer acquires; a dollar at the retention desk only defends — and teaches the customer to come back for more.
  • ✓Cap agent discretion, fund the base. Narrow BTL authority; redirect the budget into in-market offers and bundle attach.
  • ✓Measure it. BTL credit as a % of ARPU becomes a reported internal metric, not an invisible leak.
◈ The CEO line: "We run two price books and only one of them is honest. The retention desk quietly hands out twenty to sixty dollars a month to whoever threatens to leave, while the customer who never complains pays full freight. That is not a pricing strategy, it is a tax on loyalty — and now that the regulator makes us tell every customer when their promo expires, the whole base is about to learn the trick. My answer is the five-year price lock: one price, published, held. Collapse the gap before the market collapses it for us."
◈ BTL credit ranges ($20–60/month, 6–24 months) reflect widely reported retention-desk practice across Canadian carriers, not a TELUS-disclosed figure. Carriers do not publish BTL economics. Treat as directional market context.
